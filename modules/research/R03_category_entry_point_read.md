# R3 · Category Entry Point Read

**Module:** Research · **Version:** 1.0 · **New in DEOS Open** (built from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's published method and DEOS practice)

> **Find the situations in which buyers think of the category, then measure which brands come to mind in each.**

## When to use

- You need to know why a brand is or is not chosen, beyond "awareness."
- Planning messaging, media, packaging, menu or range: deciding which buying situations to own.
- Diagnosing a brand that is known but not bought, or bought by too few people.
- B2B and trade buyers too: a distributor rep or a kitchen manager has entry points just as a shopper does.

## What a Category Entry Point is

A **Category Entry Point (CEP)** is a cue that buyers use to reach into memory when they are in a buying situation. It is the thought that comes before the brand: "something warm to grab on the way to work," "food for a team lunch," "a snack my kids will eat in the car."

A CEP is **not a place to buy.** "At the gas station" is a channel. "Need something filling while driving" is an entry point.

The method was developed by Jenni Romaniuk and Byron Sharp at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science. The idea behind it: brands grow mainly by being easy to think of (mental availability) and easy to buy (physical availability), in as many buying situations as possible, across as many category buyers as possible.

## The standard

### 1. Generate entry points from buyers, not from the brand team

Use the **7 W's** as a prompt list, from Romaniuk's published work:

| W | Prompt |
|---|---|
| **Why** | What need or goal starts it? |
| **When** | What time of day, week, year or life stage? |
| **Where** | Where are they when the need arises (not where they buy)? |
| **While** | What else are they doing? |
| **With / for whom** | Alone, with whom, or for whom? |
| **With what** | With what other food, product or tool? |
| **hoW feeling** | What mood or state are they in? |

Rules:

- **Elicit first, in the buyer's own words.** Open-ended questions or interviews with category buyers: "Think about the last time you bought [category]. What was going on?" Do not show them a list first. A phrasing you supplied is not a phrasing you found (see R7).
- **Generate wide, then cut.** Start with 30 or more candidates. Tracking studies usually carry no more than about 15.
- **Run elicitation twice, independently,** and compare the two lists. If the overlap is low, your list depends on who you asked or how. A simple control: measure the overlap (shared items divided by all distinct items). Set the bar before you run, for example 50%, and if it is missed, add a third pass before trusting the list.
- **Write each CEP as a buyer situation,** never as a brand benefit. "When I need to feed a crowd quickly," not "Fast and convenient."

### 2. Prioritize entry points by how often they happen

A CEP is worth owning in proportion to how many category buying situations it covers. Ask category buyers which situations apply to them and how often. Rank by prevalence. A small but distinct CEP may still matter if no competitor owns it, but say that is the reason.

### 3. Measure which brands buyers link to each entry point

Use **pick-any association:** show each CEP and ask which brands, if any, come to mind. Buyers may pick none, one or several. Survey **category buyers**, not just your brand's buyers. Including non-buyers of your brand is the point.

### 4. Report four metrics

| Metric | What it is | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Mental Penetration (MPen)** | % of category buyers who link the brand to at least one CEP | How many buyers you are reachable in memory for at all. The headline number. |
| **Mental Market Share (MMS)** | The brand's share of all brand–CEP links in the category | Your share of the category's memory, compared to competitors. |
| **Network Size (NS)** | Average number of CEPs linked to the brand, among buyers who link it to at least one | How many situations you come to mind in, for those who think of you. |
| **Share of Mind (SoM)** | The brand's share of links among its own mentally penetrated buyers | How much you dominate memory among those who already think of you. |

These definitions follow Romaniuk's *Better Brand Health* (2023).

### 5. Read the gaps

- **MPen well above market penetration** (many think of you, few buy): a conversion or physical availability problem. Check where the brand is sold, how it is priced and how it is shown.
- **Low MPen despite strong media reach:** check that the reach was real, or whether your range and messaging actually serve the CEPs buyers use.
- **High Network Size, low MPen:** a loyal core and a narrow reach. Grow by reaching more category buyers, not by telling the loyal ones more.
- **A large CEP that no brand owns:** the clearest growth gap.

### 6. Check the supply side

Read what the brand actually says and sells: packaging, menu, product pages, sales sheets, catalog listings. For each priority CEP, mark how it is addressed:

- **Full:** the CEP is named directly in merchandising or selling language.
- **Prose:** the CEP is implied in descriptive copy but not named.
- **Proxy:** only an attribute that might serve it (a pack size, a hold time) is present.
- **None.**

Count a CEP as **addressed only at Full.** A CEP that buyers use and the brand never names is the cheapest gap to close.

### 7. Track it repeatedly

One read is a snapshot. Rerun the association measure at a fixed interval (for example, every six or twelve months), with the same CEP list and the same sample definition, so changes are real.

## How to run it

1. Write the brief (C3): the decision, the category, who counts as a category buyer, the stopping rule.
2. Run two independent elicitation passes. Check overlap against your preset bar.
3. Merge and cut to a working list (about 10–15 CEPs), written as buyer situations.
4. Field the prevalence and pick-any association survey to category buyers.
5. Compute MPen, MMS, NS and SoM for your brand and the main competitors.
6. Audit the supply side for each priority CEP.
7. Write findings: the two or three CEPs to own, the evidence, and the gap each one closes. Finish with the six-lens board (C13) and a path (C10).

## Done when

- The CEP list came from buyers, passed the overlap control, and is written as situations.
- The four metrics are reported for the brand and its main competitors.
- Each priority CEP has a supply-side mark.
- The recommendation names specific CEPs, not "build mental availability."

## Common failures

- **CEPs written as brand benefits** ("premium quality"). Those are attributes, not situations.
- **Channels mistaken for CEPs** ("at the c-store").
- **Surveying only your own buyers.** MPen needs all category buyers.
- **One elicitation pass.** You cannot tell whether the list is real or an artifact of the session.
- **Reporting awareness and calling it mental availability.** Awareness is "have you heard of it"; MPen is "do you think of it when the need arises."
- **Too many CEPs in the survey.** Buyers tire; the data gets noisy.
