# M3 · Working Backwards

**Module:** Marketing & Brand · **Version:** 1.0 · **From DEOS:** cm_85 §09A

> **Start at the decision-maker's own definition of winning and work back to the first action. Your work is a link on that chain, not the start of it.**

## When to use

- Writing a plan, proposal, budget request or brief for a boss, a client or a partner.

## The standard

1. **Take their words, not a paraphrase.** Ask what winning looks like. Note the version they volunteer after the official one; it is usually the real one.
2. **Build the chain backwards, one link at a time.** Each link answers: what has to be true for the link above? Stop when you reach something that can be done this quarter.
3. **Place what they asked for on the chain.** People ask for the thing they can picture. It is often a real link and rarely the lever. Showing where it sits reframes the brief without contradicting them.
4. **Name the links that are not yours.** Capacity, pricing, operations, legal, their data. A chain with no links outside your control is built to sell, not to win.
5. **Name the cheap links first.** If a link can be done faster or cheaper by someone else, or by them, say so and sequence it first.
6. **Show the path to their stated goal,** even if you think it is the wrong goal. If you see a better path, put it beside the first at equal weight, and name the one question that decides between them. They decide.
7. **Defend the budget against the chain,** not against your effort.

**The tripwire:** if your last two plans had no link marked "not mine" or "cheaper elsewhere," the method has become a sales funnel.

## How to run it

1. Write their success statement, in their words.
2. Build the chain backwards to this quarter.
3. Mark each link: mine, theirs, someone else's, cheap.
4. Place their request on it.
5. Write the stated-goal path. Add the alternative path if you have one, and the deciding question.

## Done when

- The plan opens with their chain, in their language.
- At least one link is marked as not yours.

## Common failures

- **Starting from your service list or your team's capabilities.**
- **Telling an experienced operator their goal is wrong** instead of showing both paths.
- **Burying the disagreement** in an appendix.
