# M13 · The Sell-In Story

**Module:** Marketing & Brand · **Version:** 1.0 · **New in DEOS Open** (built from external research on foodservice and convenience-store distribution, and DEOS practice)

> **A product sold through distributors has three buyers before it has a customer: the distributor, the distributor's sales rep, and the operator. Each needs its own reason, and getting listed is not the finish line.**

## When to use

- Launching or growing a product sold through foodservice distributors, convenience-store distributors, brokers or buying groups.
- Writing a line review deck, a distributor pitch, a sell sheet, or a sales-rep incentive.
- Any route to market where the people who stock it are not the people who use it. The same logic applies, with different names, to B2B resellers and channel partners.

## Who is in the chain

| Party | What they do | What they want |
|---|---|---|
| **Manufacturer** (you) | Makes the product, funds programs | Volume, distribution, margin |
| **Broker** | Sells on your behalf to distributors and operators, for a commission. Does not buy, store or deliver. | Easy wins across their portfolio; products that do not take much of their time |
| **Distributor** | Buys, stores, sells and delivers. Broadline distributors carry thousands of items across categories; specialty and regional distributors carry fewer. | Turns, margin, low handling cost, proof of demand, fewer stock-outs and fewer dead items |
| **Distributor sales rep (DSR)** | Sells to operators on the route, often on commission. Often the most influential voice in what an operator buys. | Items that are easy to explain, earn commission and solve a real problem for accounts they already have |
| **Group purchasing organization (GPO)** | Pools operators' buying to negotiate price and rebates. Common in healthcare, education, contract dining and independents. | Contracted items that save members money |
| **Operator** | The kitchen, restaurant, cafeteria or c-store that uses or sells the product | Food cost, yield, labor, consistency, menu fit, less waste |
| **End customer** | Eats or buys it | The occasion it serves (see R3, M8) |

Operators usually prefer to buy from the distributors and contracts they already use. So your product must be **available where they already buy,** and must give the DSR a **reason to raise it.**

## The standard

### 1. Map the route to the plate first

Before any pitch, write the chain for this product: which distributors, which brokers (if any), which segments, whether a GPO contract applies, and who decides at each step. Name a person or role at each link. Use R1 (Map the Ground First) for the people, and R2 (Category Read) for the fixed dates: line reviews, distributor food shows, menu cycles, bid seasons.

### 2. Know the four numbers

You cannot pitch a link in the chain without knowing what it earns. For each segment, write down:

1. **Your sell-in price** to the distributor (after allowances).
2. **The distributor's margin** on top. This varies widely by distributor, category and segment. Get the real figure from the distributor or broker; do not plan on a guessed range.
3. **The operator's acceptable price point:** what they will pay per case, and per portion.
4. **Landed cost per portion against what the operator uses today.** This is the number the operator decides on.

If the operator's cost per portion does not beat or justify itself against the incumbent, no story will save it.

### 3. Write three stories, not one

**The distributor story** (for the category manager or buyer at line review):

- Proof of velocity: sales from another distributor, a pilot, or operator commitments.
- Margin per case and per pallet slot.
- Handling: case size, pallet configuration, shelf life, storage (dry, cooler, freezer).
- What it replaces or adds: will it pull sales from an item they already carry?
- Your pull-through plan (section 6) and the funding behind it.

**The DSR story** (for the people on the route):

- One sentence a rep can say to an operator.
- Which accounts it fits: segment, menu type, kitchen setup.
- Why switching is worth the operator's trouble.
- What the rep earns: a sales incentive or contest if you fund one.
- Samples they can leave behind.

**The operator story** (for the chef, kitchen manager or store manager):

- **Yield and portion:** servings per case, waste, trim.
- **Labor:** prep steps and time removed. In a short-staffed kitchen this often decides it.
- **Cost per portion** against what they use now.
- **Menu versatility:** how many dishes or dayparts it serves.
- **Holding and waste,** especially for hot or prepared food: how long it holds at quality, and what happens to the unsold product.
- **Equipment fit:** does it work with what they have?
- **Allergen and nutrition** information, ready to hand.

### 4. Know where the money flows

Programs that move money through the chain, and where each one bites:

| Program | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Off-invoice allowance** | A discount taken off the distributor's invoice | It may not reach the operator's price |
| **Rebate / bill-back** | You pay back after proof of sale | Paperwork, delays, and claims you cannot verify |
| **Deviated pricing** | You fund a special operator price; the distributor bills you back the difference. Common with chains and large accounts. | Paying twice: a GPO rebate and a distributor deviation on the same case |
| **Marketing development funds** | Money for distributor promotions, shows and catalogs | Spending with no link to sales |
| **Slotting or listing fees** | Paid to get an item set up | Less common in foodservice than in retail grocery, but they exist |
| **GPO contract and rebate** | A contracted price or rebate for member operators | Overlap with other programs |

**Rule:** for each program, write who receives it, what it is meant to change, and how you will check it changed. Audit for duplicate payments on the same case.

### 5. Make the item ready before you pitch

A distributor cannot list what it cannot set up. Have ready:

- A product identifier (GTIN) and case and pallet data in the format your distributors use. GS1 US publishes foodservice data standards.
- A spec sheet: ingredients, allergens, nutrition, case pack, dimensions, weight, shelf life, storage, preparation.
- Pricing by segment and program.
- Food safety and certification documents the distributor requires.

### 6. Plan the pull-through

**Getting listed is not the finish line.** An item that is listed but not sold gets cut at the next review. Plan, and fund, what makes operators order it:

- **Start narrow:** one distributor, one or two segments, one geography. Prove velocity there before expanding.
- **Train the DSRs:** a short session, a one-page sheet, a sample.
- **Sample operators:** cuttings, demos, ride-alongs with DSRs.
- **Show up at the distributor's food show,** if it has one.
- **Give DSRs a reason to sell it:** a funded incentive, tied to new accounts or cases, with an end date.
- **Track by account:** which operators bought, and whether they reordered.

### 7. Measure at 30, 60 and 90 days

Set targets before launch (R9): new operator accounts, cases per week, reorder rate. At each checkpoint, report against the target. A listing that does not hit its numbers needs a changed plan before the next line review, not after.

### 8. Convenience-store foodservice is its own case

Prepared food is a large and growing share of c-store profit. NACS reports that foodservice made up 28.5% of in-store sales and 38.9% of in-store gross profit for U.S. convenience stores in 2025, with prepared food among the highest-margin categories. That makes the store's questions sharper:

- **Hold time and waste:** how long it holds at quality in the case or warmer, tested the way a store actually runs (door openings, temperature swings). Waste is the store's biggest cost in prepared food.
- **Labor:** can a store employee prepare it with little training?
- **Daypart:** which part of the day does it win (morning, lunch, afternoon, late night)?
- **Equipment and space:** does it fit what is already in the store?

## How to run it

1. Map the chain with named roles (section 1).
2. Fill in the four numbers for each segment.
3. Write the three stories.
4. List every money program and check for duplicates.
5. Get the item data ready.
6. Write the pull-through plan and its budget.
7. Pitch one distributor and one or two segments.
8. Report at 30, 60 and 90 days. Decide: expand, fix or exit.

## Done when

- Each link in the chain has a named role and its own story.
- The four numbers are filled in from real figures, not guesses.
- The pull-through plan is funded and dated.
- 30/60/90-day targets are written before launch.

## Common failures

- **One deck for everyone.** The distributor, the rep and the operator want different things.
- **Treating the listing as the win.** Unsold items get cut.
- **No cost-per-portion comparison** against what the operator uses now.
- **Paying two programs on the same case.**
- **Going wide too early,** with no proof of velocity to show the next distributor.
- **Ignoring labor and waste,** which often matter more to an operator than the case price.
